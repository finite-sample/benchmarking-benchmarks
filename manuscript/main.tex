A benchmark is a measuring instrument. Its score is useful only if evidence supports the interpretation and use we attach to it. Benchmark datasets such as MNIST and ImageNet have stimulated work by providing agreed-upon marks to beat, but a dataset is only one part of the instrument. The task, object being evaluated, target population, items and labels, split, metric, administration protocol, and decision rule jointly determine what a score means. When these parts are underspecified, it is hard to explain why the best-performing model varies across datasets, compare models, or forecast performance on new data \citep{dehghani2021benchmark,bouthillier2021variance}.

Principles developed for educational and psychological measurement provide a useful starting point \citep{aera2014standards}. Models are not people, and benchmark items do not reveal a human trait. The useful analogy is narrower. A model-development procedure produces responses to items under stated conditions, and a benchmark turns those responses into a score. Measurement theory asks which claims that score supports, what variation counts as error, and whether the proposed use follows from the evidence.

\section*{Start with the intended interpretation and use}

Validity is not a permanent property of a test, dataset, or score. It is the degree to which evidence and theory support a proposed interpretation for a proposed use \citep{cronbach1955construct,messick1995validity,kane2013validating}. A benchmark can therefore be valid for comparing two fixed procedures on a frozen dataset and invalid for claiming that one procedure is generally better, or valid for research comparison and invalid for deployment.

An interpretation/use argument for a benchmark has at least four links:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Scoring.} The rule that transforms predictions into a score preserves the performance feature of interest.
  \item \textbf{Generalization.} The score generalizes across the admissible examples, datasets, annotators, training runs, prompts, and occasions.
  \item \textbf{Extrapolation.} Performance in the benchmark domain supports a claim about the target population or deployment setting.
  \item \textbf{Decision.} Using the score to select, reject, or deploy a model improves the decision under the relevant costs and harms.
\end{enumerate}

Evidence for one link does not validate the others. Correct labels do not establish extrapolation. A representative sample does not rescue an arbitrary metric. A predictive relationship does not establish that a deployment rule is acceptable.

The cat-dog example illustrates the point. If all dog photographs are taken outside and all cat photographs inside, an indoor-outdoor classifier may perform best. That score may predict outcomes in a population with the same association. It does not support the broader interpretation that the model distinguishes cats from dogs without using setting as a shortcut. The setting creates construct-irrelevant variation. A dataset containing too few breeds, poses, or occlusions may instead underrepresent the construct.

Population and loss are also part of the claim. A benchmark for name-based demographic inference can pool available labeled datasets, as in large-scale evaluations of name-to-gender inference \citep{krstovski2023gender}. Its overall score then describes the curator's mixture of sources unless that mixture represents a stated population. Suppose instead that the task is to predict the Census race or Hispanic-origin category of a person selected at random from the 2010 United States population, given only a surname represented in the Census tabulations. The tabulated conditional distribution supplies the Bayes-optimal hard classification under zero-one loss. Change the population, year, name fields, categories, or loss, and the optimal rule can change \citep{uscensus2016surnames,laohaprapanon2025ethnicolr}.

\section*{Build a validity argument}

Measurement theory organizes evidence by the claim it probes, not by a flat checklist of desirable properties.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Content evidence.} A benchmark needs a blueprint that defines the construct's facets and explains how the items represent them. For text classification, facets might include topic, text length, label structure, production channel, language variety, time, and named distribution shifts. A probability sample supports population prevalence. Deliberate coverage of rare but important cases supports a different claim and should be reported separately.

  \item \textbf{Evidence about response behavior.} The benchmark should test whether outputs respond to construct-relevant changes and remain stable under irrelevant changes. The indoor-outdoor example is a rival hypothesis about what produces the score. Black-box tests can reject some behavioral implications of a claimed process, but they do not by themselves identify a model's internal reasoning.

  \item \textbf{Internal-structure evidence.} Item and subscore relationships should match the claimed structure of the capability. If performance is strongly multidimensional, one total score hides rather than measures the differences. Item clusters, repeated templates, and shared source documents also violate independence and reduce effective sample size.

  \item \textbf{Relations with other measures.} A score should converge with defensible measures of the same capability and remain distinct from measures of different capabilities \citep{campbell1959multitrait}. Agreement among benchmarks that share sources, templates, or labeling artifacts may reflect common method variance rather than the construct.

  \item \textbf{Consequences.} Benchmark use changes research behavior. It rewards some tasks and metrics, encourages optimization against public test sets, and may invite selective reporting. These consequences matter when a score is used to claim general progress or allocate attention \citep{dehghani2021benchmark}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Make scores dependable}

Reliability is broader than agreement among labelers. Generalizability theory defines a universe of admissible observations and estimates how score variance changes across its facets \citep{cronbach1972dependability}. Relevant facets for model evaluation include tasks, examples, annotators, label aggregation rules, training runs, prompts, demonstrations, decoding settings, software, hardware, time, and benchmark form.

The benchmark should state which facets are fixed and which are sampled. A score on a fixed set of datasets supports a claim about those datasets. A claim about new tasks needs a defensible task universe and between-task uncertainty. A generalizability study can decompose the variance. A decision study can then ask whether another dataset, another training run, or more examples would most improve precision. Choosing a large round test-set size without locating the dominant error source is not a design.

The required precision depends on the use. Ranking two procedures is a relative decision. Certifying that a procedure exceeds a deployment threshold is an absolute decision. The same benchmark can be dependable enough for one and not the other. Reports should include uncertainty from test sampling and from stochastic training or model selection when those sources are part of the object being compared \citep{bouthillier2021variance}.

Label reliability is one part of this analysis. Agreement statistics must match the label scale and sampling design. Low agreement may reveal unclear instructions, ambiguous cases, plural but legitimate judgments, or an infeasible task. Majority vote, averaging, and adjudication define different targets; none automatically produces truth \citep{artstein2008agreement}. Test-set label errors can be common enough to alter rankings \citep{northcutt2021labelerrors}.

\section*{Implications for benchmark construction}

These principles imply an order of work.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the score interpretation, intended use, object being compared, and decision.
  \item Define the construct, target population, loss, and universe of admissible observations.
  \item Write a blueprint for construct facets, content coverage, item formats, and prohibited shortcuts.
  \item Establish provenance, consent where applicable, privacy protection, licenses, and permitted uses. Public accessibility is not blanket authorization \citep{gebru2021datasheets,longpre2024provenance}.
  \item Specify labels, instructions, annotators, aggregation, scoring, metrics, thresholds, and treatment of abstentions.
  \item Draw a representative population sample for the main performance claim. Use identified oversamples for subgroup precision and separate purposive samples for rare or critical capabilities.
  \item Pilot items for ambiguity, difficulty, discrimination, dependence, label error, and construct-irrelevant sensitivity. Exact and near-duplicate entities should not cross splits \citep{barz2020duplicates}.
  \item Run a generalizability study and allocate items, datasets, annotators, and repetitions according to the variance components and precision target.
  \item Standardize preprocessing, tuning access, prompts, external data, compute, decoding, and metric implementation. Maintain immutable versions, contamination records, and an explicit linking design if scores must be compared across versions.
  \item Publish a validation dossier stating the evidence, rival hypotheses, uncertainty, intended uses, and unsupported uses. Data statements and datasheets supply useful documentation templates \citep{bender2018datastatements,gebru2021datasheets}.
\end{enumerate}

This sequence also changes how examples should be discussed. An AG News story about a Massachusetts sales-tax holiday can plausibly be called Business news. Without the category definition and decision process, a user cannot tell whether the label is an error or an intended boundary case \citep{zhang2015character}. ImageNet's review of its person subtree found offensive, sensitive, and non-imageable categories and led to the removal of more than 600,000 images. The defect involved the construct and its consequences, not merely inconsistent coders \citep{imagenet2019update}.

\section*{Where behavioral tests fit}

CheckList organizes behavioral tests by linguistic capability and by minimum-functionality, invariance, and directional-expectation tests \citep{ribeiro2020checklist}. In measurement terms, minimum-functionality tests add targeted content evidence. Invariance tests probe construct-irrelevant sensitivity. Directional-expectation tests probe whether a construct-relevant change moves the output as specified.

These tests belong in a benchmark when they probe a claim in its validity argument. They should be mapped to the blueprint, validated by people, versioned, and analyzed at the level of independent template families. They should ordinarily remain a diagnostic profile rather than be averaged into the population score. A templated failure rate is not a population error rate, and black-box behavior is not direct evidence of an internal response process. Aggregation requires weights tied to deployment frequency, utility, or a prespecified decision rule.

\section*{Research agenda}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rank portability.} Define a universe of text-classification tasks, evaluate fixed model-development procedures under a common protocol, and estimate the probability that the winner on one task wins on a new task. Report winner-transfer probabilities, rank correlations, and selection regret with uncertainty. A generalizability study should separate variation across task families, tasks, items, and training runs.

  \item \textbf{Selective benchmarking.} Test whether papers disproportionately report eligible benchmarks on which their proposed procedure has a relative advantage. Reported tables alone cannot answer the question because performance on omitted benchmarks is missing by construction. The design must define eligibility without consulting outcomes and recover performance for a sample of reported and unreported eligible pairs.
\end{itemize}
